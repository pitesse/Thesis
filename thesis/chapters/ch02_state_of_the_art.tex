\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{ch:state_of_the_art}

\section{Overview}

The analysis of Formula 1 race strategy has progressively moved from retrospective summaries and driver/team feeling decisions toward data-driven and live-like decision support systems. 
Modern telemetry, timing data, and event logs make it possible to reconstruct the evolution of a race at fine temporal granularity, transforming each lap into a 
sequence of data events which can be analyzed and interpreted. 
In the case of pit stop strategy, this shift is particularly relevant: a pit decision depends on tyre age, gaps between drivers, lap times,
traffic, race phase, track status \& conditions, rival behaviour, and the expected post stop advantage. As a consequence, the central challenge 
is to predict whether a pit stop will occur, and also to evaluate whether a decision support system can emit useful pit calls under realistic temporal and statistical 
constraints.

This setting introduces methodological difficulties. 
First, the data is inherently sequential: the state available at lap $k$ must be processed without using information from future laps, since in a live 
scenario the information is not available. 
Second, pit events are rare compared with the total number of driver-lap observations, making standard accuracy based evaluation inadequate.
Third, the evaluation target isn't unique: predicting that a pit will happen soon and predicting that a future pit will be evaluated as successful are related but distinct 
goals. 
Finally, historical replay is useful for reproducibility, but it needs separation between event generation, stream processing, supervised learning, 
and final comparison of the results.

The state of the art reviewed in this chapter is organized around these challenges. It begins with event-time stream processing and replayable logs, which provide 
the infrastructure for reconstructing historical races as live-like streams. It then examines motorsport strategy and pit stop decision support literature, which motivates 
the race context variables involved in pit decisions. The chapter then discusses learning approaches for pit stop prediction based on race-state features, 
distinguishing between offline Batch learning and online prequential learning. 
The final sections focus on evaluation under class imbalance and temporal dependence, followed by explainability and score interpretation 
as tools for validating the methodology. This structure provides the background needed to pitch the thesis as an end-to-end replay-to-evaluation pipeline for comparing a 
Flink Strategy Engine, Batch XGBoost, and a MOA Online Learner under aligned pit-decision contracts.

\section{Streaming and Event-Time Processing}

The increasing availability of fine-grained sports timing data has made it possible to analyze races as sequences of evolving states.
In Formula 1, each lap changes the information available to a strategy system: tyre age increases, gaps to rivals evolve, track status may 
change, and previous decisions constrain future options. Pit stop decision support is therefore inherently temporal. A recommendation at lap $k$ must be based on 
the information available up to that point, rather than on the completed entire race viewed retrospectively.

This requirement naturally connects the problem to event-time stream processing. The Dataflow model formalizes concepts such as event time, windowing, watermarks, 
and the handling of out-of-order events \citep{akidau2015dataflow}. The distinction between event time and processing time is particularly important in this thesis. 
Historical races can be processed faster or slower than real time during replay, but the logic of the race must still follow the timestamps and lap order of the original
 event sequence. In other words, the system should reconstruct the causal structure of the race to mirror the actual race progression. 

Apache Flink offers an effective framework for stateful stream processing over keyed event streams \citep{carbone2015flink}. This is relevant because 
race context must be accumulated over time for each driver, race, and track-status sequence; it's not possible to reconstruct the state from individual events. 
Kafka-style logs complement this setting by providing a replayable ingestion layer, where historical events can be published again as ordered streams \citep{kreps2011kafka}. 
Together, these concepts support the core streaming data engineering requirement of the thesis: transforming past race data into reproducible live-like streams.

However, stream-processing systems only provide the computational foundation of the live-like replay. They do not define what information is strategically meaningful 
in a pit stop decision, nor how such a decision should be evaluated. This requires domain-specific race strategy reasoning, which is discussed in the next section.


\section{Pit stop Strategy and Motorsport Decision Support}

Pit stop strategy is one of the most visible and tactical decisions in circuit racing. A stop can restore tyre performance and create future 
pace advantage, but it also introduces an immediate time loss and may place the driver into traffic from slower cars. The value of a pit stop is therefore context-dependent: 
the same stop can be beneficial in one race state and damaging in another, depending on tyre degradation, gaps to rivals, pit-loss time, race phase, the circuit,
safety-car context, and the possibility of undercut or overcut moves.

Existing motorsport strategy research has approached this problem from different perspectives. Optimization-oriented work formalizes pit stop planning as the search for 
stop laps and tyre choices that minimize race time or maximize expected race outcome \citep{carrasco2023optimization,quiroga2024optimization,piccinotti2020openloop}. 
Other contributions focus on virtual strategy engineering and data-driven motorsport analytics, combining historical or live data with domain-specific reasoning 
\citep{heilmeier2020race,widanage2019indy500}. These works are important because they identify the strategic variables that should be considered when reasoning about pit stops.

At the same time, the objective of this thesis is not to compute a globally optimal race plan. Instead, the focus is on decision support from replayed race states: 
given the information available at a given lap, the system should decide whether a pit event is imminent, or whether an imminent pit event is likely to be evaluated as
strategically successful. This difference matters because the thesis is concerned with the evaluation of decision rules and learning systems, 
rather than with solving a complete race-strategy optimization problem, which may seem as a similar challenge.

The strategy literature therefore motivates the race context variables used by the system, but it does not by itself provide a comparative framework for deterministic,
Batch, and online learners. Once race context has been extracted from the stream, the problem can also be studied as a prediction over driver-lap feature rows. 
This motivates the learning-based perspective introduced next.


\section{Learning Approaches for Race-State Prediction}

The transformation of race streams into structured driver-lap observations makes it possible to study pit decision support as a supervised learning problem. 
Each row summarizes the performance of a driver at a given lap, while the target describes whether a relevant event (pit stop) occurs in a short future horizon. In this thesis, 
this formulation is used for two related contracts: predicting whether any pit stop will occur soon, and predicting whether a successful evaluated pit stop will occur soon.

This setting is naturally suited to tabular learning. The input features are heterogeneous: they may include lap-time information, tyre age, relative pace, 
race progress, gaps, and other race context signals. Gradient-boosted tree models are commonly used in such settings because they can capture non-linear interactions
 between variables without requiring the same preprocessing assumptions as many linear models. XGBoost is therefore a relevant Batch-learning baseline for this thesis 
 \citep{chen2016xgboost}. It also provides a direct path to explainability for tree models, which is useful for inspecting whether the model relies on meaningful race-state signals.

Recent motorsport machine-learning work provides broader context for the use of predictive models in racing scenarios 
\citep{sasikumar2025pitstop,hettmann2024datatopodium,dinh2025gradient}. These studies support the idea that race-state data can contain useful predictive signal. 
However, racing data also introduces validation challenges. Rows from the same race are temporally and contextually dependent, and features derived from future 
outcomes can easily create leakage if not removed from the learning matrix.

For this reason, Batch learning is treated in this thesis also as an evaluation design problem. Race-wise and race-sequential
out-of-fold evaluation are required to make learned predictions comparable to streaming decisions. This naturally leads to the second learning paradigm considered 
in the thesis: online learning under prequential evaluation.


\section{Online Learning and Stream Evaluation}

Batch learning assumes that training data is available before the model is evaluated. In contrast, online learning considers a setting in which examples arrive 
sequentially and the model may be updated over time. This is relevant for decision support over streams, where the chronological order of observations 
is part of the problem definition.

MOA is a framework for stream mining designed for online learning experiments over large or evolving data streams \citep{bifet2010moa}. An important evaluation protocol in 
this setting is prequential evaluation. Each instance is first used for testing, and only afterwards used for training. This test-then-train order preserves the 
causal constraint that the model should not learn from the same row before being evaluated on it.

Online learning is also connected to concept drift, where the statistical properties of the stream change over time \citep{gama2014drift,arora2024driftreview}. 
Adaptive methods such as ADWIN and Adaptive Random Forests are part of this broader literature \citep{bifet2007adwin,gomes2017arf}. In this thesis, this literature 
is used mainly to motivate the comparison between an offline learner trained race-wise and an online learner evaluated in stream order. The central questions are 
whether a streaming learner can predict pit targets and whether its behaviour differs meaningfully from Batch XGBoost when both are evaluated under 
aligned feature and truth contracts.

The selected online learner configuration is OzaBoostADWIN. This choice combines an online boosting scheme with ADWIN-based 
adaptation, providing a stream learning baseline that can emit per instance votes in prequential order \citep{oza2005boosting,bifet2007adwin}. 
In this thesis, the role of this learner is comparative, it is used to study how an online learner behaves under the same feature and 
truth contracts used for Batch XGBoost. The thesis does not focus on proving concept-drift adaptation itself, but on comparing offline and online decision-support 
paradigms under a shared evaluation protocol.


\section{Evaluation under Imbalance and Temporal Dependence}

Pit stop prediction is a rare event problem. In a full race dataset, most driver-lap rows do not correspond to an imminent pit stop, and an even smaller subset 
corresponds to an imminent successful evaluated pit stop. Under this imbalance, standard accuracy can be misleading: a system that almost always predicts the majority 
no-pit class may appear accurate while providing unuseful decision support.

For this reason, precision--recall analysis is more informative than accuracy or ROC-based summaries when the positive class is rare \citep{saito2015prroc,davis2006relationship}. 
Average Precision summarizes ranking quality across thresholds, while precision and recall describe the behaviour of selected decision policies. In this thesis, 
these metrics are interpreted carefully because the output of a pit decision system is a row level classification problem and also a real pit event may be surrounded 
by several nearby positive rows.

Threshold policy is also part of the evaluation problem. A model may assign useful scores, but the system must decide at which score it should actually emit a pit call.
Cost-sensitive learning motivates the idea that thresholds should reflect the relative cost of false positives and false negatives \citep{elkan2001costsensitive}. 
In pit strategy, false positive advice can be costly because an unnecessary stop may damage track position or compromise the race. This motivates the use of precision weighted 
measures such as $F_{0.5}$ and the explicit analysis of threshold sensitivity.

Additional metrics are useful for inspecting behaviour under imbalance. Cohen's Kappa measures agreement beyond chance \citep{cohen1960kappa}, while G-Mean captures 
the balance between positive and negative recognition \citep{he2009imbalanced}. In this thesis, these metrics are used to complement precision--recall analysis and to 
inspect the behaviour of the system under different decision thresholds.

Temporal dependence creates a second evaluation challenge. Rows from the same race share track conditions, event history, and strategic context, 
so random row level splits can overestimate generalization. Structured validation is therefore important when examples are grouped or temporally correlated 
\citep{roberts2017cvstructured}. Leakage risks are particularly relevant when target derived variables, future outcomes, or matched event markers are accidentally 
included in the feature matrix \citep{brookshire2024leakage}.

The evaluation protocol used in this thesis builds on these concerns and introduces a accounting distinction specific to the domain. Positive decision rows are evaluated 
at row level for precision, while real pit events are counted at event level for coverage. This prevents repeated positive rows around the same pit event from being 
treated as multiple independent successful detections.


\section{Explainability and Score Interpretation}

Explainability is used in this thesis both as an interpretability tool and as a methodological diagnostic. In predictive systems based on race-state features, 
it is not sufficient to know that a model produces a useful score. It is also important to inspect whether the model relies on plausible strategic signals or on 
shortcut variables that encode dataset structure.

SHAP provides a general framework for additive feature attribution \citep{lundberg2017shap}. For tree ensembles, TreeSHAP provides an efficient and consistent 
attribution method \citep{lundberg2018treeshap}. This is particularly relevant for Batch XGBoost, where explanations can be computed directly from the trained tree model. 
In this thesis, SHAP is used both to describe feature influence and also to detect the role of season identity as a shortcut risk.

Score interpretation is a related but separate issue. A score can be useful for ranking examples even if it is not a calibrated probability. 
Probability-quality literature highlights the distinction between discriminative ranking and calibrated probability estimates \citep{niculescu2005goodprob,guo2017calibration}. 
This distinction is especially important for the MOA Online Learner, whose vote scores are used for threshold frontiers and precision--recall diagnostics but are not treated 
as true probabilities without calibration.


\section{Positioning of This Thesis}

The reviewed literature provides the individual building blocks for this thesis. Stream-processing systems provide event-time 
execution, stateful computation, and replayability. Motorsport strategy research identifies the variables and trade-offs involved in pit stop decisions. Batch and online 
learning provide alternative ways to learn from race-state data. Imbalance aware evaluation and validation designed to avoid leakage define the methodological constraints needed for 
trustworthy comparison.

The contribution of this thesis is to combine these elements into a reproducible pit-decision evaluation pipeline. Historical races are replayed as live-like streams, 
processed by a Flink Strategy Engine, converted into auditable data lake artifacts, and used to compare deterministic rules, race-wise Batch XGBoost, and a MOA Online 
Learner under aligned contracts.

The thesis therefore focuses on two questions. First, whether pit timing can be learned from historical race-state patterns. Second, whether successful pit call prediction
can be evaluated under a stricter operational contract that treats no-match calls and failed-pit calls as false positives while keeping unresolved outcomes out of the clean 
training and evaluation universe.
